# A character with a life of their own

Offscreen RPG is a game about living a life that continues to matter after the moment it is narrated. You create a character, shape their intentions, encounter other lives, and return to a world that remembers what actually happened. A shift at a remote outpost, a long walk, a conversation that changes a relationship and a dangerous confrontation can all become equally meaningful.

The game does not need to manufacture dramatic events whenever you look at it. Some lives are quiet. Some are chaotic, tragic, funny or frightening. A selected Storyteller and campaign direction shape that experience; the engine protects the rules, consent, persistence and consequences that make any of those styles believable.

## A life is earned, not announced

An intention is not its completed result. Beginning an eight-hour journey does not put the character at their destination. Agreeing to a paid shift does not grant the wage immediately. Possessions, injuries, promises, opportunities and relationships matter because actions, chance, time and prior decisions have made them real.

Real elapsed time can therefore be part of play. A long action may take hours at the campaign's chosen pace. Ordinary work can finish quietly, or a meaningful development can interrupt it. Completion, interruption and its results are durable. When the player returns days later, the world has neither forgotten the work already done nor fabricated work that was never authorized.

Time is not a tax on clicking. A brief exchange may resolve promptly; when fiction and supported mechanics require duration, that duration is explicitly admitted and accounted for. The [time-and-autonomy product contract](../../../../docs/product/gameplay/time-and-autonomy.md#pace) governs accepted execution, pauses and absence. In particular, **closing a browser does not by itself authorize a new action or cause fictional time to advance**. An unattended choice may be made only when a separately selected delegation policy and the player's specific consent permit it.

The value of persistence includes possible loss. A character's five days of effort should not be protected merely because they took five days. When the campaign permits severe injury, theft or death and the fiction supports that outcome, those consequences can occur. Risk must still be legible enough for meaningful choices; hardship is not an excuse for arbitrary punishment.

## The rules make the world real

D&D-inspired abilities, skill checks, dice and recorded consequences are fundamental, whether the character is fishing, arguing, travelling, working or fighting. The Storyteller can judge an attempt and propose an outcome, but the application resolves supported mechanics, commits the result and prevents a later narration from rerolling it or creating the same reward again. The selected [gameplay rules](../../../../docs/product/gameplay/rules.md) define the first supported subset; full edition fidelity and elaborate combat are separate design decisions.

The fictional setting is not forced into one body's assumptions. A wizard may own equipment and cast spells. A microorganism may have no backpack, speech or humanoid attributes. A disembodied mind may have no current use for physical Strength. Rules remain meaningful when applicable; inapplicability does not mean unlimited capability. The [worlds and characters contract](../../../../docs/product/gameplay/worlds-and-characters.md) owns those expectations, and technical capability design must respect them.

## The Storyteller creates possibilities, not unauthorized truth

The Storyteller is a creative identity realized through configurable, purpose-specific work, not necessarily one continuous agent or model conversation. Its profile shapes voice, rhythm, severity and style. A campaign may also give it permission to cultivate private narrative direction, introduce new pressures or remain content with ordinary life.

It may prepare a scene, propose choices, reveal a development or bring back a person whose earlier actions matter. A character might spend the afternoon fishing while a distant conflict grows. Equally, a deliberately quiet campaign may never require a grand plot. The engine must not impose a preferred density of action or an inevitable final confrontation.

There is a boundary between imagination and authority. A private possible invasion is not yet an invasion. A description of a sword is not a granted sword; a mention of a traveler is not automatically a registered actor. Mechanics, player decisions, costs, committed state and private/public knowledge are checked by the application. The selected [Storyteller behavior contract](../../../../docs/product/storyteller/behavior-and-quality.md) governs what it may do and what it must refuse. Campaign creative controls, canon-invention ceilings and risk permissions remain distinct from model price or context size.

## Remember what matters; do not simulate everyone

A lived world contains more people and details than the application needs to store. It is not necessary to invent an individual daily schedule for every farmhand beyond the hill. New places and characters can be described naturally; consequential identities, relationships, events and possessions acquire stable evidence when later consistency depends on them.

The character who borrowed your coat, the innkeeper who heard a promise and the tool you sold must not conveniently vanish from memory when the conversation buffer fills. Published prose, admitted mechanical receipts and canonical world records have distinct roles. Recaps and summaries help retrieval; they do not rewrite history. The [continuity product contract](../../../../docs/product/gameplay/continuity.md) and [Storyteller memory contract](../../../../docs/product/storyteller/memory-and-continuity.md) define the difference.

This is why a sparse world can still feel alive. We preserve **significant continuity**, not a census of imagined life.

## What the player experiences

The browser opens into the current story, with its atmosphere, relevant people and possessions, readable prose and the choices actually available. You can make an intention, see it admitted, leave while an accepted activity proceeds, receive a development and later inspect a coherent chronology. The same surface must show holds, uncertainty and stale choices honestly.

The first [Living Book experience target](../../../../docs/product/experience/living-book-poc.md) uses a stable reading object and bounded scene expression. It is not approval of final typography or art direction. Notifications can bring a player back, but are not an alternative authority for current state or an obligation to interrupt the player continuously.

The game aims to support solo life and, eventually, friends controlling different characters in a shared story. Shared decisions, group pause authority and generalized delegated choices need their own ratified policies before they become required first-playable behavior. The maintained [acceptance scenarios](../../../../docs/product/gameplay/acceptance-scenarios.md) illustrate both current proof boundaries and longer-term stress cases; the implementation evidence and release milestone live in STATUS and the selected work program.

## The smallest convincing proof

The first convincing slice is not an elaborate open-world simulator. It is a coherent experience in which a player creates a character, selects an authorized intention, sees an immediate consequence or genuinely waits for a long action, and later returns to persistent facts they can trust. One quiet interval and one meaningful interruption teach more about the product than a hundred generated paragraphs with no durable consequences.

The project also serves as a playable, hosted full-stack engineering portfolio proof. A visitor should be able to understand the basic premise and encounter a meaningful decision promptly, without waiting through a whole work shift during their first session. This demonstration convenience must not secretly rewrite the semantics of longer real-time play. Exact first-release scope, sponsored provider funding and hosted readiness require their own accepted and evidenced contracts.

Larger shared worlds, many participants, elaborate map simulation, deep combat, richer media and new contact channels are future possibilities. They must grow from the same foundations: a stable source of truth, player agency, time and consequence.

## The standard against which to judge it

Ask whether a normal day can remain uneventful without becoming meaningless. Whether an item or relationship acquired last week remains real today. Whether a change of intention can invalidate an uncommitted future scene without erasing the past. Whether a character unlike a human adventurer can still act and suffer meaningful consequences. Whether the game can let a chosen Storyteller be quiet, unpredictable or severe without changing the mechanical rules behind the player's back.

If a new subsystem makes those experiences harder to maintain, it may be making the implementation larger without making the game better.

## Reviewer assessment before adoption

Compare this draft claim by claim against [the actual canonical page](../../../../docs/product/foundations/vision.md), not against a remembered summary. Record lost clauses, scope changes and implicit permissions. In particular review the first-playable/shared-play sentences, the D&D backbone, life-ending risks and the distinction between poetic “ongoing life” and actual accepted execution. The migration would be an **editorial replacement**, not automatic approval of unresolved multiplayer, generalized delegation, first paid budget or production deployment.

**Success is not word-count reduction.** It is preservation of the distinctive product while allowing a new reader to locate its binding rule and its open boundary without translating old ticket chronology.
